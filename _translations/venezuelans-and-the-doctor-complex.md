A joke is often the expression of a deeper state of mind than one sometimes thinks. A joke serves, for example, to express and vent resentments, to give free rein to low and unruly tendencies in certain sick minds with their characteristic obsessions, and, most often, to ridicule a situation that is very serious, or at least serious for those who are living it.

Venezuela is a country where jokes abound. Teasing, the famous *mamadera de gallo*, is an everyday affair, at every hour and in every setting. Some in one way, some in another, from the most refined to the coarsest in their ways of living, everyone uses and suffers the weapon of the joke. Well, and this is where I was going: not long ago I heard a joke that clearly reflected a serious national problem. It said (I heard several versions, by the way, some of them unprintable) that Venezuela was a country of incalculable resources: specifically, it told how the Creator, when He distributed natural resources among the countries of America, had been exceptionally generous with ours; but that when He came to the people... (let us stop telling the joke here).

What is raised here, and vented through the joke, is honestly one of the great national problems: Venezuela lacks enough trained people to carry forward its programs of national development. Venezuela asks only for hands, and trained minds, and a spirit of service and dedication: she provides the rest. And the problem is acute. Either we overcome this crossroads, which arises in social, political, economic and religious life, or we will go on as we are today, living on the dream of a glorious country, a dream whose chances of coming true will grow ever more remote. Our vital need, then, is to form people, to develop to the full all the human potential of our young people. If we succeed in forming our youth, we will then be able, with a spirit of *initiative*, to reach the great goals of land reform, industrialization, literacy and raising the living conditions of this poor people who own a rich country.

But what has happened? How is it that we have neglected this problem so much that over the years the achievements have been so poor? Should we blame the generations that came before us? Or the oligarchic governments? Or the demagogic regimes? No, the problem is much more complex, and its root lies in the very heart of our society.

The problem began many years ago. Several generations ago. Let us state, then, what in our view is the root of this ill: *In Venezuela there is a "doctor" complex.* In Venezuela young people are forced to go to university. In Venezuela people are made to feel, think and live for academic degrees. What we have sketched here, stated haltingly, is what we intend to discuss in this article.

Let us begin by setting down a principle of vital importance. Let us say that *education is for man, and not man for education.* That is, education, that vital right of human nature, is a right meant to serve man, and in no way to enslave him. Man (and this is where his right comes from) is a rational animal. And as a rational being he has the right to fulfill himself within the bounds of a rational nature. A man who is denied the chance to educate himself is, to a large extent, being denied his very humanity. He is being condemned to be a beast, a good deal weaker than bears, though perhaps a little more skillful than elephants. Therefore, and we must state the conclusion clearly, precisely and sharply: to deny in theory, or what is worse, in practice (even while accepting it in theory), man's right to education is to deny him a primary right inherent in his human nature. For this reason we must condemn those oligarchic political regimes that, knowingly or not, neglect the education of the people and keep most citizens in a lamentable state of ignorance. Those sorry rulers of the public destiny do not serve the State; they serve themselves from it.

However, no one has said that university education is a natural right of all people, without distinction; much less that it is the best instrument for forming the great majority. Man has the right to fulfill himself as a man, and education is an instrument for achieving that fulfillment. University education, then, will help some people fulfill themselves and will lead others (there are real cases of this) to frustration. Whoever has a natural bent for intellectual work should pursue it. *And he should serve his country, society and God with his intellect!* On the other hand, whoever has the abilities, even exceptional ones, to be a technician, one of those makers of our modern civilization, should develop these human qualities of his to the fullest, since being his own they are instruments of fulfillment and of service. But our education system does not allow it. Nor does the "doctor" complex we mentioned at the beginning of this article.

Here, through secondary school and social pressure, young students are pushed into university classrooms. All of them, except those who have clearly realized that their professional calling lies elsewhere (teaching, for example) and who resolutely follow their own path, which bears fruit through their greater dedication and professional enthusiasm. And we might ask: what is the aim in pushing everyone to university? To brand people as cattle are branded, without those years of study serving them for anything but feeding bitter complexes and thankless frustrations? Is this the mission of our education system?

The country needs technicians. It needs people trained, with a proper career, in the industrial arts. Only with them, through their effort, can we build a prosperous Venezuela. This is the fundamental excellence of technical careers in our country: they, the technicians, must be the builders of a better social order and better living conditions. For this is not achieved with mere theorizing, but with a high level of technical skill! They must be the engine of industrialization and development. Then the farmer will have the means to produce to the full. And the ordinary man will be able to live in keeping with his dignity as a man. And the poor, that vast human capital of people buried by hunger and lack of training, will see their lives raised to a human standard in material terms. They will have homes fit for human beings, and children fed with food fit for human beings. But the excellent role of technical careers does not stop there: they must be an instrument of fulfillment, of human completeness, in the lives of so many people who are now being channeled down paths that are not their own. The person called to serve with technical intelligence will find, in being trained for it, the same deep happiness that the one called to serve as an intellectual may find in crowning his university studies.

Questions of justice are at stake in planning education. Justice toward the country, and toward its people, who have the right to be happier and to live better. A deep review and a prompt diversification of the ways we form people are therefore essential. More than that: within our society, within our homes, we need to review the meaning we are giving to education. So that a father does not pressure his son to earn a doctorate or not, but encourages him in his personal struggle to develop his human qualities to the fullest. That is how we must build our Venezuela: with its people serving one another, because each has found the place, the post that is theirs in life.

*Rafael Tomás Caldera P.*
