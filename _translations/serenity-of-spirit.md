In the first century of our era, Seneca left us a set of points for assessing how we are doing in serenity of spirit. I thought it would be interesting to describe them very briefly and in today's language, especially with an eye to the lack of serenity that overwhelms us, the real or imagined pressure that almost all Venezuelans are suffering these days. These are six very practical points that can help us see life with more perspective and bring a little more quality to our relationships and decisions. Serenity lets us face any kind of problem with maturity.

The first point of "self-assessment" is to know whether we have some time to think: whether we have a few minutes to reflect calmly on our words, our actions and the way we handle our social and family relationships. Human life is woven around the unexpected, the unforeseen, the unsuspected. That is why it is important to ask ourselves: Is what I am aiming for something one can reasonably expect in life? What means must I use to reach those ends? What difficulties and obstacles will I run into? If we acted otherwise, life would keep surprising us and we would end up crashing headlong into reality.

The second point concerns the need to be detached from status symbols, that is, from the markers of prestige and social and economic position, or in simpler words, from "consumer goods". The harm of this excessive attachment, according to Seneca, begins when these goods become the north of our immediate aspirations, when what are merely tools become first-rank values among our motives. Besides, excessive or unbounded concern for status symbols breeds "envy" and "daydreaming", leading to an unstoppable inner restlessness to get the gadget, the car, the brand, and so on.

The third point concerns mastering "restless laziness", which does not mean inactivity but failing to do what must be done at the moment it must be done, since laziness can go hand in hand with busyness or apparent activity. Laziness feeds on the whim of the moment, on disorder, on not ranking things by importance, and on unpunctuality.

The fourth point invites us to banish sadness, which leads to a kind of heaviness of spirit, a shrinking of the self into itself that paralyzes action and brings on drowsiness, yawning, listlessness, weariness and boredom. Whoever gives in to sadness seeks refuge in the "pleasure" of playing the victim, of lamenting, complaining and reproaching... We have a right to be sad when we suffer, but we must try not to let immoderate sadness flood us and wrap itself around us.

The fifth point is the art of resting. But it refers to rest after tiredness, and its purpose is to restore our strength so we can get tired again... Logical, isn't it? Moreover, whatever rest we take should not damage, weaken, interfere with or break the habits, dispositions and customs we already have. So it is not about sleeping twenty-four hours or doing absolutely nothing, but about changing activity: an activity that takes less effort, but is orderly and enjoyable.

Finally, a fundamental point is suggested: to have a good friend. Seneca says that nothing balances the spirit so much as a sweet and faithful friendship. Man's essentially social nature must grow and find exercise in the business of everyday life: to know ourselves as human we need the close experience of loving and being loved, of crying and laughing together, of standing by others wherever we find grief, sorrow or disappointment. With a friend, shared sorrows seem smaller and shared joys lift everyone's spirits.
