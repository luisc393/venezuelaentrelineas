Educating our seven million young people is a demanding task for the whole community, and one without precedent. Here the idea of community means accepting that education is not only the job of the country's 370,000 teachers. It is everyone's task and everyone's problem. It is a problem for the "whole city". It is a service offered by the State and by private institutions, and it is also shaped by families, by political authorities, by the mass media and by every other organization that sets out to form its members.

At first sight, all this seems clear and even taken for granted. But I have the feeling that there is no "working conviction" (shared by the whole nation) that this is really so. First of all, we should remember that education is a good that is offered and handed to another person as something of value. It therefore fits perfectly into what we might call a "service". Here, however, I must add that education is not a service like the one a travel agency provides, nor is it a service for improving or maintaining health. Education can be understood more deeply as a "service of spiritual improvement". It is the spirit of young people that we improve, or leave stagnant (or even make worse), when we try to educate them. It is in the spirit of those young people, in their feelings, their ideals, their attitudes and aptitudes, their knowledge, that we forge a change.

*Educere*, the Latin word from which "education" comes, means to draw out, from the inside outward. Young Venezuelans need to be understood from "within". Within them lies the love they will one day have for their country. Within them lies the value they will give to knowledge, to study and to honest personal growth. Within them lies how much they will prize democracy and freedom; their attitudes toward human relationships; their understanding of what a human being is, or ought to be. Within them we set fortitude, patience, order, sincerity, perseverance, creativity and a true love of work. Within them we can help them become capable of giving themselves generously to others.

The problem is delicate. But the honesty and clarity with which we, as a community, understand what it means to educate can change our convictions and our actions, so that we face reality more genuinely. And so, in agreement as a community, we can change the narrow view that educating is simply passing on knowledge and lessons. Our young people are not educated by giving them repetitive, rigid knowledge to memorize. Nor are they educated with sermons or lectures, even though a very good lecture is needed now and then. Our young people are educated by thinking of each one of them, with a first name and a last name, with particular parents and a particular situation. Our youth are educated by thinking about what goes on in their hearts, by taking an interest in what interests them.

The responsibility is immense. The 370,000 teachers in the system cannot do it all. It is true that they must keep digging into "how" to do it better. But parents and politicians also educate through their example; they shape the values that young people are forming. Companies and their cultures, with their managers and the way they treat workers, shape a person's educational experience. And some media, without a doubt, with the way they handle public opinion, distort the facts, present "false idols" and introduce "values" that have nothing to do with our culture, sadly disturb and even hold back the true and necessary education of what must be formed from within young people.

We all educate. We are all responsible for the overall quality of this service of spiritual improvement. If we want a better country, if we want the "spirit" of Venezuela to be better and more mature, if we want to know how to face the problems we have now and those that lie ahead, we must think about education as a responsibility of the whole community. And the first people who must change the way we understand and offer education are each one of us, in the place where we move and live. A difficult task, but a necessary one.
