*[Illustration: drawing of an open door with a flowerpot on the threshold.]*

They say that the gate of heaven is narrow and the gate of hell is wide. So, in this door of Angostura, which opens today onto the Guayana of El Dorado, I want to see a promise like the promise of heaven.

The Orinoco narrows its waist in front of Ciudad Bolívar; for a long time it had been waiting to fasten on the belt of the bridge, not only to join opposite banks but distant regions: the Venezuela of the North, already in full development, and the Venezuela of the South, still half asleep under the green mane of its forests.

In the heart of every Venezuelan, whatever their home region, there should be deep rejoicing at the opening of the Angostura bridge. With it, a wealth that is still dormant, and that holds great potential for the country's development, begins to awaken.

The bridge is the door to El Dorado. No longer the absurd legend that led the conquistador's ambition to failure, but a vast region rich in natural resources that is about to be developed.

But something more than an awakening of material wealth must be stirred there if the Guayana of El Dorado is to fulfill its destiny. While thousands of men devote themselves to building the region's economy, alongside them and for them the transcendent constructions of the spirit must also rise. Then El Dorado will stop being a legend.

I began this "door" very seriously, and very seriously I meant to finish it. But I have been slow in writing it for health reasons (some battered vertebrae have forced me to rest, and from my bed, with the typewriter almost on the floor, the index finger of my right hand taps slowly and on and off. I get tired). I have had time to follow the press commentary on the opening. What a fiasco, friends! My door has no choice but to abandon its seriousness once again. I can no longer speak seriously about such a serious fiasco; I would feel "like a cockroach at a dance of hens", and with my vertebrae in bad shape I cannot set about changing this barnyard surf rhythm.

A quick summary: a cardinal is not let through and arrives late. A president begs for a glass of water, and when he gets it, a first lady has to hold it for him throughout the speech, to drain it herself (parched, under the blazing sun) as soon as her husband finishes speaking. If the country's first two citizens were in that state, better not to ask how everyone else was doing. Throats fit for "Lawrence of Arabia". A dancer is arrested because supposedly she rudely ordered an altar or a throne removed (the report is unclear) so that she could rehearse, and although the story turned out to be gossip, the governor showed incomprehensible violence toward a sublime performer. Who, by the way, behaved with great nobility of spirit: Yolanda Moreno danced in Angostura even though she had her first and only jail cell there, in a hotel room.

Luckily, the President thought of cutting his losses and ending the ceremony early. He probably thought he would end up with a heap of diplomats roasted in front of so much water, and wanted to avoid an intervention by the United Nations. It is not very common for diplomats to die of thirst at the opening of a bridge. But it would have been original. A cruel joke for history.

In short, the momentous opening of the Angostura bridge turned into a farce. Now we've really done it: we Venezuelans have taken up vaudeville. And here I was, planning to say such lovely things (or so I think) about the legend of El Dorado, about the poet's soul of the Indian who invented it, about the historical meaning of the name Angostura. Oh, world!, as the people of Barquisimeto say, and I have their blood, all this poetry is resting in the inkwell (what am I saying, in the typewriter ribbon), and I remain very truly yours, hoping to open another door soon.

*Alicia Álamo Bartolomé*
