Months ago I read a news story I could not believe: the police in Rotterdam found the body of a woman who had been dead in her home for 10 years without anyone noticing. Listen carefully: not 10 days, or 10 weeks, or 10 months, but 10 years! I checked online to see whether they had made a mistake, but no! I found several links with the same story. The report said the body was discovered by the city police after a tip from some construction workers. Most incredible of all, the neighbors said they had not noticed anything, and that they thought the woman had gone to live with her daughter. And I ask: how could the daughter not have realized that something had happened to her mother?

The story also said that the national fund for the elderly in the Netherlands warns that such cases will become more and more frequent, because the trend is for older people to want to live at home for longer and longer, and only the very sick usually move into care homes. I have always thought there is nothing sadder than living in a home for the elderly. I know, and I understand, that every case is different, and that sometimes children decide to place their parents there, but I believe it hastens their death, because loneliness hits hard at that age.

The sad part is that the story cited a study by a health organization in Amsterdam: every day the body of someone who has been dead for more than 24 hours is found in the city, and five times a year someone is found more than two months after dying. In other words, these people die alone. Worse still, this does not only happen in that part of the world. A friend of mine who had to move to the United States told me that old people's lives there are very lonely: children leave home very young, rarely visit their parents, and many times the parents die completely alone.

The lack of humanity in the world has reached astonishing extremes. Several months ago I also read a story I will never forget: a 70-year-old Albanian man placed a death notice in a newspaper announcing his own death, to see whether that way he could bring his family together. In an interview with a newspaper in his town, the man said: "Four and a half years ago I came back from Canada, and neither my children, nor my brothers, nor my cousins have come to have a coffee at my house. While I was in Canada I helped everyone with money as much as I could. I really wanted to come back to my homeland and live with my loved ones. But now that I'm here and I have no money left, nobody pays any attention to me." Not even that last effort to gather his family was well received, because only his eldest daughter came to "his funeral". After that experience, the man said that on the day of his real death he will allow no one at the cemetery except the gravedigger.

How sad it is to see old people alone! We are seeing it here too, especially now that so many children have had to emigrate. But thank God there are many people here who, despite all the problems they may have, lend a hand when there are lonely or needy people close by. I know several cases, but the most exemplary is that of a friend whose neighbor is a lady of about 70 who in turn has a daughter with Down syndrome. That lady was recently diagnosed with Alzheimer's disease, and her other son (who lives abroad) calls her and scolds her because she forgets things. My friend has taken charge of that lady and her daughter, because the son cannot even send her money. It is a very sad case, but an exemplary one too, because my friend has become like a devoted daughter to that lady.

Pope Francis has spoken many times about caring for the elderly. Recently he pointed out that the only commandment that comes with a promise is the Fourth Commandment: "Honor your father and mother, that it may go well with you and that you may live long on the earth" (Ephesians 6:2-3). "Today more than ever," says the Pope, "the Church (and all of us who are baptized are the Church!) must set an example for the whole of society that the elderly, despite their inevitable 'ailments', sometimes serious ones, are always important; indeed, they are truly indispensable. Beyond any discriminating outlook, human life always keeps its value in God's eyes. For this reason the elderly take full part in the mission of the Church, because they carry with them the memory and wisdom of life, to hand it on to others."

May God grant that there be not a single old person abandoned in this world. Caring for our parents and grandparents is more than a duty of justice; it is a joyful commandment, because there is nothing more beautiful than serving them and keeping them company, even when it costs time and sacrifice. God bless all our dear old folks!
