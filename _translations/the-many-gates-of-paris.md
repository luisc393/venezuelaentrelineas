**From Luxembourg**

*[Illustration: Paris façades and rooftops, with chimneys and antennas.]*

You can reach Paris from every possible direction: by air, by land, and by the river too. France's vast network of roads converges on the great capital, and these routes are the endless tentacles of an octopus that draws every vehicle and traveler toward itself. That is why it is hard to leave Paris: all the roads seem driven by a centripetal force toward the Île-de-France that is difficult to escape. You want to get out, or to slip past the monster at a tangent, and every sign says, with monotonous insistence: PARIS. We travelers cannot take our eyes off the road maps; we look for lodgings, for a detour, so as not to go back into the place we have just left, and at the slightest slip, the smallest unplanned turn, we find ourselves heading for the capital again. We had gone to Versailles after saying goodbye to Paris, and then we wanted to head for Reims. Escaping that pull toward the center was a hard and painful task; it was an unequal struggle between four bold women travelers in a little Renault van and the monster with a thousand tentacles. It took us hours to get past it without being devoured, swept along in the whirlwind of traffic. We could not reach Reims that night; we had to stay over in Meaux, but at last we were free, the last tentacle weakened by the kilometers. The peaceful night of the provinces gathered up our weariness.

And what lies behind those countless gates of Sèvres, Orléans, Saint-Cloud and many more? A gigantic city that is the goal of the world. The rich go to Paris in search of luxury, pleasure, good food, fine clothes and great shows. The poor go to Paris after saving up year after year, in search of a little of the same (whatever their means can reach), or in search of the light of culture and art that the generous capital can offer. In the streets of Paris, the races and types in their immense variety no longer surprise anyone. We know we will find everything. Not even love, which for us always carries a sign of intimacy and modesty, hides there. It is in plain sight, on the very skin of the city. In the Latin Quarter, where the Sorbonne and Notre-Dame rise to vie with or harmonize the forces of the spirit, the world's young people are a strong majority: for those of us already passing the last stretch of our prime, walking along the boulevards of Saint-Germain or Saint-Michel means feeling a little older. And if we have the misfortune, or the good fortune, of going alone, the feeling grows deeper. All we can do is reflect a little on these young people who bump into us in their hurry. I did.

What strange youth! Strange, perhaps, to those of us whose youth was different, simply because it may be the mark of youth always to be surprising and new. A youth in which the boys have long hair and many of the girls short. Hair that merges with beards, and thank God for that in quite a few cases, since it allows us to tell the sexes apart. A youth with a pattern of exotic appearance.

We go down into the tunnels of the Métro, and there the young people are still exchanging kisses; they have good means of brightening a journey that we solitary ones must endure as it is, amid shoving, stuffiness and hurry. Where are we all going, like moles scurrying through the caverns of the Métro? From time to time we come up into the light of Paris at a station, but soon our destinations send us back down to seek them in those depths. Direction Neuilly, direction Porte d'Orléans, direction Balard. Changes of line, to Vincennes, to Clignancourt, and we go on burrowing like moles through tunnels lined with white, dusty tile.

A youth running after its pursuits: study, culture, pleasure, love. And others of us, not so young, who out of curiosity or for a rest (a rest?) have fallen, innocent or guilty, into the headlong life of this capital.

There is something Paris has lost these days: the sacred, very French tranquility of mealtimes. At least in the inexpensive restaurants (if anything in France can be called inexpensive), because they are always full and at peak hours you have to wait for a seat. "Self-service" systems have even appeared, which seem very out of place in this civilization of fine dining. But it is easier to eat there when you cannot find a place elsewhere. You go along with your tray and choose your dishes, and there is the added advantage that you don't leave a tip, which until now has been another of the country's institutions.

Nowadays every average Frenchman has a car; this prosperity is due to the many benefits the Common Market has brought. That is why traffic is now a nightmare. Paris has very wide streets but lacks parking; finding a spot for the *voiture* is a real ordeal. You end up stopping anywhere, blue zone or not, and we tourists stare at the tickets on our windshields like hens staring at salt.

In spite of everything, Paris is still the city of attraction. Its art, its culture, its thousands of famous shows and that *savoir-faire* of all things French still intoxicate us, and whoever lives in Paris falls in love with Paris. But Parisians are not very fond of foreigners, and they have a point: we make their life more expensive. Every millionaire or artist feels obliged to spend long seasons in Paris, buy an apartment and dress in the great fashion houses. Dollars turn into francs, and everything costs more than in any other country.

And Paris is beautiful. Now that several parts of its face have been washed, it appears in a new light to those of us who have seen it several times. The Place de la Concorde, with the pinkish yellow of the stone of its noble buildings brought out among the lights and fountains at night, has something like a brilliant rebirth of imperial times. That chunk of Greek coal that was the Madeleine has become clear, clean Hellenic beauty. The gilding of the Opéra stands out against the whitened stone.

We are leaving Paris once more, but we must return. The octopus with a thousand tentacles has only granted us a truce; the pull toward its center, though it seems imperceptible now, keeps working, and we will fall into it, inevitably, before we say goodbye to Europe.
